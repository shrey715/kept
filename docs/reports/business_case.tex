\makereporttitle

\statrow{\stat{\$1.55M}{a year, base case (\$0.44M–4.3M)}\stat{2.4×}{year-one ROI, base case}\stat{\textasciitilde 2.6 mo}{to pay back the build}\stat{+2.7 pts}{promised money paid, measured on randomised cases}}

\takeaway{\textbf{The short version.} Collections loses money in one place: cases that \emph{roll} into a worse bucket.
The cheapest way to stop a roll is a promise the customer can actually keep. Measured on the bank's own randomised
cases, within days-past-due bands, dating every promise to just after payday would raise promised money paid by
\textbf{2.7 points} (95\% CI 0.1 to 6.1; \path{docs/reports/evidence.md} §2). Kept dates promises to payday where it
knows or can ask for it, swaps letters for near-free digital nudges, and
gives agents and QA reviewers time back. Counted conservatively, that's \textbf{\$1.55M a year} against
\$150K to run and \$300K to build, so it pays back in under three months. Every number below comes from the release, except seven assumptions
that are named, explained and stress-tested in §7.}

\section{Where the money leaks}

\fig[0.8\linewidth]{bc_funnel.png}{Cases that entered collections at 1–30 days past due in the last 12 months, and what happened next.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} Of the 194,515 cases that entered at 1–30 days past due in the 12 months to 28 Sep 2026
(\$63.9M overdue), about half roll into a worse bucket within 30 days (48.8\% across 330,779 cases since 2016). Of the
rolled cases, \textbf{2.5\% end up charged off, at \$12,523 on average}; of the cases that \emph{don't} roll,
\textbf{none} do. Rolling is the only road to a charge-off, so everything Kept does is aimed at the first 30 days.

\section{The lever: timing the promise}

\fig[0.8\linewidth]{bc_payday.png}{Resolved promises by days between the customer's last salary deposit and the due date.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} Both panels slope the same way: the further a due date sits after payday, the worse the
promise does. Due 0–2 days after payday, \textbf{54.3\%} of promises are kept and \textbf{62.5\%} of the promised money
arrives. Due 8+ days after, when the pay is already spent, that drops to 46.3\% and \textbf{51.5\%}. The money panel
moves more than the count panel, because a late-dated promise that does get paid is more often paid only in part.

\paragraph{From the chart to dollars} The chart is descriptive: part of the gap is \emph{who} gets offered which
date. So the money uses the effect \textbf{measured on the bank's randomised cases}, within days-past-due bands: dating
every promise to just after payday raises the promised money paid by \textbf{2.7 points} (95\% CI 0.1 to 6.1). On the
\textbf{\$41.7M} promised in the last 12 months, for the 55\% of promises whose payday Kept knows or is told, that's
\textbf{\$0.62M} a year. The 5\% hold-out measures the real effect after launch.

\insight{How Kept uses this}{Kept sizing is plain arithmetic, not a model: the due date is 1–2 days after the expected
payroll date (detected from salary deposits), and the amount is split into up to three instalments that each fit inside
the customer's spare cash for that pay period. A promise that can't be sized that way isn't proposed at all.}

\section{A cheaper, kinder contact mix}

What changes is \emph{which} calls. The current strategy's biggest bar is collection calls (34,326); Kept's is

\section{The upside we don't count: roll rate}

From §1: 194,515 cases a year enter at 1–30 days past due, 2.5\% of the ones that roll get charged off, at \$12.5K each.
So \textbf{every point of 30-day roll rate is worth about \$603K a year} in charge-offs avoided, before counting the
collection cost of the rolled cases. Our design hypothesised 1–2 points.

We keep it \textbf{out of the base case}: a kept promise \emph{is} what stops a roll, so counting both would count the
payday effect twice. The high case adds one point.

\paragraph{Why it's cheap to run} One nightly batch scores the whole book in about seven minutes on a single
machine. Every language-model call, the Ask included, runs on a local open model on one GPU: no per-call fees, and customer
text never leaves the bank. DuckDB and dbt replace a cluster. The \$150K is mostly one operations engineer (\$120K) plus
about \$30K of GPU server and warehouse compute. The \$300K build is four engineers for three months.

\section{Same payments, fewer calls}

Who gets a call matters more than how many calls are made. On the 141,350 decisions after 15 August that the uplift
model never saw, calling every one of the 42,369 call candidates brought no more payments within 14 days than waiting
(25 fewer). Calling only the model's top 42\% brought \textbf{1,446 more payments}. Stopping there leaves about 24,600
calls unmade, about \$59K in contact cost each pass. Actions were chosen by the bank, so this compares what happened rather
than a trial, and it is not corrected for who the bank chose to contact (the portfolio page shows the curves, from
\path{data/exports/reports/model_card.json}). A doubly robust re-check on a clean outcome (account-sourced payments,
\path{docs/reports/uplift_improvement.md}) finds the call ranking adds less than calling every call decision, so treat the
saving as an upper estimate; an earlier comparison with the bank's recommendation field is withdrawn, because that field is
look-ahead.

\section{Break-even, and the chance of paying back}

\textbf{Break-even.} The year-one cost is \$450K (build plus a year's running). At base, the contact mix, wrap-up time and
QA time alone bring in \$0.93M, so \textbf{Kept pays back in year one even if dating promises to payday adds nothing}.
With every one of those at its low end as well, it needs a payday effect of \textbf{0.2 points}; the measured effect is
2.7 points [0.1, 6.1] (evidence §2).

\textbf{The chance of paying back.} Instead of three scenarios, draw every assumption at once, 20,000 times: each from a
triangular distribution over its low, base and high values, and the payday effect from a normal distribution on its
measured interval (so a zero or negative effect is possible).

\fig[0.9\linewidth]{bc_simulation.png}{Annual benefit across 20,000 draws of every assumption. The line is the year-one
cost, build plus run.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} The median annual benefit is \$1.77M, and 19 draws in 20 fall between \$1.13M and
\$2.50M. \textbf{99.97\% of draws pay back build and run within year one.} The draws treat the assumptions as independent,
which narrows the spread. The strictest check runs the same draws with every assumption moving together (all high or all
low at once): 19 in 20 then fall between \$0.64M and \$3.45M, and \textbf{98.5\% still pay back within year one}. The
low scenario (\$0.44M) is the corner where every assumption is at its worst at the same time.

\section{Risks, costs we're honest about, and how we'll know}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Specialist workload.} 38,187 of the 96,000 cards (40\%) were Red, and every one carried a hardship
signal: 25,914 from the case's own hardship flag, 12,273 from what notes, calls or the customer said, and 9,865 of them
also marked vulnerable. A specialist reviews each Red card before anything is sent. That is the protection working, but 7,367 of
them were customers whose hardship plan was still running, where a new review repeats the last one. Kept now skips
the review while a plan runs (the card says it is monitoring the plan, and every protection and hard stop still
applies): Red cards fell to 33,646, about 750 specialist hours a pass saved at 10 minutes a review.
\item \textbf{Not yet a trial.} The payday effect is measured on randomised cases and checked three ways (evidence
§2a), but the due dates themselves weren't randomised, so it is still an estimate. A permanent \textbf{hold-out} stays
on the old strategy and a \textbf{kill switch} reverts everyone if the effect isn't there. How long the hold-out needs
(80\% power, 5\% significance, 3,720 new 1–30 day cases and 2,720 promises a week):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{To detect} & \textbf{5\% hold-out} & \textbf{10\%} & \textbf{20\%} \\
\midrule
Promises kept +4.6 pts (the measured effect) & 7 weeks & 4 weeks & 2 weeks \\
Promises kept +2 pts & 35 weeks & 19 weeks & 11 weeks \\
Roll rate −2 pts & 28 weeks & 15 weeks & 8 weeks \\
Roll rate −1 pt & 111 weeks & 59 weeks & 33 weeks \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So the plan is four weeks in shadow mode, then a 20\% hold-out for eight weeks, which shows the payday effect within
two weeks and a 2-point roll-rate drop by week eight, then 5\% once it is proven.
\item \textbf{What isn't in the numbers.} Fewer complaints and less regulator risk from finding hardship when the customer
says it rather than weeks later; fairness checked on every run; a plain-English reason and an audit trail for every
decision. Those are hard to price and matter anyway.
\end{itemize}
