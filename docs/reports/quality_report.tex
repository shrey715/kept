\takeaway{\textbf{The short version.} The release is messy on purpose, and most of the mess is about \emph{identity}: five
systems, five customer keys, dates you can't read reliably, phones you can't use. We built the golden record by
linking what we can prove first, matching the rest probabilistically, and leaving anything ambiguous empty instead of

is safe to publish downstream.}

\section{How we checked}

Every check in this report is a query over the release or the warehouse built from it; nothing is estimated or typed
in by hand. The pipeline has four stages, and each one has its own checks.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Land raw, clean after.} Bronze keeps every file exactly as delivered, every column as text. The release
README warns that \code{NA} is a real outcome code and that some IDs carry leading zeros; both survive (all 228,124
\code{NA} contact outcomes are intact).
\item \textbf{Small, tested cleaning rules} for names, dates, phones, postal codes, provinces, snapshots and the renamed
column. A value a rule can't read is \emph{never guessed}: it's left empty and written to a quarantine table with the reason.
\item \textbf{Four layers of checks:} dbt tests on every model (types are enforced on gold), Soda checks generated from
the data contract, the contract gate that blocks publishing, and a person for what code can't decide.
\end{itemize}

\section{The release at a glance}

\fig[0.78\linewidth]{q_rows.png}{Records per table (log scale). Five tables hold three quarters of the rows.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} The data is dominated by history: loan instalments, monthly account snapshots,
transactions and card statements together hold 47.3M of the 68.2M records. The tables that matter most for
\emph{identity} are small by comparison (customers, cards, loans, deposits, cases: under 4M rows together), which is why
matching could run on one machine. One detail already hints at the identity problem: \code{customers} has
\textbf{1,020,000 records for 1,000,000 people}.

\fig[0.92\linewidth]{q_nulls.png}{Average share of missing values per column, the twelve emptiest tables.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} High numbers here are mostly \emph{by design}, not defects. \code{contact\_history} is
52\% empty because its 128 columns describe many different event types, and each row only fills the ones for its
event. A charge-off date only exists for charged-off accounts; a reversal ID only for reversed transactions. So the
missing-value profile tells us the shape of the data. The actual problems are in the next section.

\section{The issues, and what we did about each}

\fig[0.95\linewidth]{q_issues.png}{The issues that touch the largest share of their table.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} The three biggest bars aren't errors in the usual sense; they're traps for a naive pipeline:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{64.7\% of autopay / PAD accounts are at other banks.} Matching on them would link strangers who happen to
pay from the same external account number. We never use them to link.
\item \textbf{58.3\% of card phone numbers are masked} (\code{519555xxx42}). Phone is useless as a card match key, so
Splink leans on name, date of birth and postal code for cards.
\item \textbf{34.9\% of customers carry the National Do Not Call flag.} It covers telemarketing, not collections. Treating
it as a contact block would wrongly silence a third of lawful servicing contact.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Schema drift} \code{kept map} compares the columns every raw table arrives with to the data dictionary. It

\section{Do the tables actually join?}

\fig[0.92\linewidth]{q_links.png}{Share of child rows whose key finds no parent row. Red: worth a look.}

\textbf{What the chart says.} Notes, transcripts, promises and agent references join perfectly. Two joins deserve a closer
look:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Case → CRM record: 10,365 cases (2.82\%) point at a CRM ID that isn't in \code{customers}.} This was the
one real surprise. Those cases aren't lost: their card, loan and deposit keys, plus Splink, still put them on a person,
and the gate checks that every \emph{open} case resolves to a golden ID.
\item \textbf{Contact → case: 26,864 orphans, exactly 1.00\%,} right at the tolerance of the release's own example
contract. They're kept and counted but join to nothing.
\end{itemize}

The autopay and PAD bars are tall on purpose: most of those accounts are at other banks (§3).

\section{Identity resolution}

This is the step that can actually hurt a customer: a wrong merge means calling someone about a stranger's debt. So it
gets the most checks.

\subsection{How keys become people}

Two passes. First, \textbf{exact rules} link keys we can prove: the case's copy of the CRM ID, autopay/PAD to our own
deposit account, the same national-ID hash, CRM's own duplicate pointer, and the core-banking customer number (CIF)
shared by deposits and the bureau file. Then \textbf{Splink} (Fellegi–Sunter matching on name, date of birth, phone and
postal code) scores what's left. A score of 0.95 or more links automatically, 0.80–0.95 goes to a data steward, and
anything lower stays apart. A cluster with two national IDs or more than 12 keys is never merged automatically.

\fig[0.92\linewidth]{q_rules.png}{Source keys by the rule that linked them (log scale).}

\textbf{What the chart says.} Splink does most of the work (2.04M keys, 53\%), but the exact rules carry the backbone:
the CIF rule alone links 892K keys, and it wasn't in our original design; we found it in the release README and checked
that name, DOB and postal code agree on 99.7–100\% of those pairs. The national ID, the field you'd expect to be the
strongest, links only 33.8K keys, because most records don't carry one.

\fig[0.85\linewidth]{q_sizes.png}{How many source keys one person has (log scale).}

\textbf{What the chart says.} The typical person has \textbf{three to five keys} (921K of 1.05M people), which matches a
CRM record plus one or two products plus a case. 64,092 keys stay single. The tail stops at 12 keys by design; seven
larger clusters were held for review rather than merged.

\paragraph{Splink is decisive} Every key Splink auto-linked scored 0.98 or higher, and only 251 pairs fell into the
0.80–0.95 band that goes to a steward. The model rarely sits on the fence, which makes the human review sample (next)
the main check on whether it's \emph{right}, not just confident.

\subsection{The human check}

The organisers gave no ground-truth IDs, so a person reviewed 300 randomly sampled predicted links, sorted into tiers by
how many identifiers agree (276 clear, 14 check, 10 look closely).

\insight{Why the two errors matter}{Both wrong links (MP-238, MP-239) are Splink pairs where everything matched
\emph{except the surname}, and both scored the maximum, 1.0. No threshold would have caught them. That's the argument
for keeping a reviewed sample in every release, and for the rule that a cluster holding two national IDs is never merged.}

\paragraph{Recall, by injection} Precision says the links we make are right; recall says we make the links we should.
The CRM's own duplicate pointer can't measure it (the matcher uses it as an exact rule), so we planted 200 copies of real
customers with no national ID and no pointer, each damaged one way (exact copy, surname typo, no phone, new postal code,
first initial only), and re-ran the matcher. \textbf{198 of 200 (99\%) merged with their original}; the 2 misses stayed a
person of their own (no wrong merges), and 2 of 50,036 other clusters shifted. Details: \path{docs/reports/identity_recall.md}.

\section{Beyond the golden record}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Text.} All 760,594 notes and 25,000 transcripts are redacted (English and French spaCy, Canadian SIN and
postal codes, mangled cards and dates of birth) before any model reads them. A full scan afterwards finds \textbf{0}
leftover identifiers, and the LLM gateway refuses anything that isn't redacted.
\item \textbf{Labels: public only.} Text models are tuned and tested only on the release's 500 public labels per source,
30\% held out. The remaining labels belong to the organisers.
\item \textbf{Features.} Training and live values agree on all 56,000 compared cells. The drift report covers how
tonight's features compare with training.
\end{itemize}

\section{What we can't measure}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{True match accuracy.} There's no ground truth. The human review and the exact-rule cross-check (Splink
re-finds 99.9\% of the links the exact rules prove) are the evidence.
\item \textbf{Monthly roll rates.} The account snapshot only has quarter-ends (Dec 2023 to Jun 2026), so roll rates are
quarterly.
\item \textbf{Generated fields.} The release README says some fields were set from generation rules; we take them as given.
\end{itemize}

\section{If we owned the source systems}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Carry one customer ID, or at least the CIF, in CRM: 892K of our links depend on a number only deposits and the
bureau hold.
\item Force one date-of-birth format at entry: 12.7\% of CRM dates of birth are ambiguous today.
\item Stop masking card phone numbers in the extract.
\item Publish schema changes (\code{channel\_v2}, the overdraft columns) in the dictionary before they ship.
\item Keep a 300-link human review per release: it caught the only two bad links.
\end{enumerate}
