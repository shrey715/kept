Kept: a guarded next best action for promises customers can keep

This page explains the whole system in plain words. It starts with the people who use Kept, follows what they type or click all the way to the final result, and then opens up every layer it passes through: what it does, how it is built, why it helps, the numbers that prove it, and the questions a judge might ask.

The problem, in one minute

What collections does

When a customer misses a card or loan payment, the collections team contacts them, agrees a plan and recovers what is owed. Done well, it helps the customer get back on track. Done badly, it harasses someone who is already struggling and still doesn't get paid.

Why it is hard today

  • One person, many records. The same customer exists in CRM, cards, loans, deposits and the collections system, each with its own ID and slightly different details.
  • The important facts are in free text. "Lost shifts, can pay 50 on the 15th" sits in an agent note or a call recording that no system reads.
  • Generic treatment. The same reminder goes out at the same time to everyone, often due before payday, so promises are made that can't be kept.
Our answer. Kept (1) unifies the data into one trusted record per customer, (2) lets anyone ask the data questions in plain English, and (3) recommends the next best action for each case: safety rules first, then a model that predicts who will actually pay because we contacted them, then a promise sized to the customer's payday and spare cash. A person reviews and releases every action.

How this maps to the challenge brief's three layers: Layer 1 (Data Product Factory) = layer 1 below. Layer 2 (Insight and NLP) = the Collections Ask. Layer 3 (AI Decisioning) = text, features, decisioning and human release. Governance runs through all of them (layer 7).

Who uses Kept

Seven roles, each sees only what their job needs (role-based access). Names are the demo personas.

AK

Collections agent Aisha

Works a queue of cases. Opens a case, sees the recommended action and why, talks to the customer, types a note, and releases everyday actions (calls, promises, plans) on her own cases.

MS

Hardship specialist Maya

Handles customers in financial difficulty or who are vulnerable. Approves the sensitive (Red) actions first, such as starting a hardship review.

TL

Team lead Tom

Releases the low-risk (Green) reminders in a daily batch, and acts as the second person on Red actions.

EP

Compliance Elena

Reads the full audit trail, can be the second approver, and is the only role that can see protected attributes, and only to check fairness.

NK

Analyst Noah

Asks questions of the data in plain English ("what was the promise-kept rate in August?") and gets an answer with the SQL behind it.

SA

Data steward Sara

Reviews customer matches the system isn't sure about ("are these two records the same person?") and approves or splits them.

And the customer. They never use the app, but everything is built around them: no contact outside their calling hours, no more than the weekly contact limit, a promise they can afford, and a hardship review instead of pressure when life goes wrong.

The journey: from what the user does to the final result

    Aisha opens a case from her queue Layer 5 →

    Case CS-2026-820205. The card already shows last night's recommendation: "Send an app reminder", a Green action, with three plain-English reasons, the calling window in the customer's own time zone, and every safety check ticked.

  2. 2

    On the call the customer explains, and Aisha types a note

    "spoke w/ cust, lost shifts at the plant since Aug, can only pay 50 on the 15th"

    This is the input. Nothing in the old system would ever read it.

  3. 3

    Personal details are removed first Layer 2 →

    The redactor (Presidio, English and French) replaces names, phone numbers, card numbers, SINs, dates of birth and addresses with tags like <PERSON>. Only redacted text can be passed to any AI model: the code refuses anything else.

  4. 4

    A local AI model reads the note, about 5 seconds Layer 2 →

    Gemma 3 (12B), running on our own GPU (no data leaves the machine), returns structured facts, and must quote the exact words behind each one:

    hardship: possible ← "lost shifts at the plant since Aug" · promise: $50 on the 15th ← "can only pay 50 on the 15th"
  5. 5

    The customer's hardship status updates Layer 2 →

    The golden record goes from "none" to possible hardship. Only a person can make it "confirmed" or clear it.

  6. 6

    The decision engine re-runs, rules first Layer 4 →

    Hard stops→Permissions→Hardship override→Uplift model→Promise sizing→Rank→Reasons + tier

    Because hardship is now possible, only protective actions are allowed. The card flips to "Call to start a hardship review", a Red action, with a banner naming the hardship programmes the customer is eligible for.

  7. 7

    Two people release it Human in the loop →

    Maya (hardship specialist) approves first. Tom (team lead) approves second. It must be a different person. At release, every safety check runs again on fresh data: if the customer asked us to stop in the meantime, the release is blocked.

  8. 8

    Final output: a released, explained, audited action

    The audit log holds the recommendation, the hardship change with its quote, both approvals, and who did what and when. At wrap-up Aisha confirms or corrects the extracted facts; her confirmation is what lets a "cease contact" request become a hard stop.

  1. 1

    Noah types a question Layer 6 →

    "What was the promise-kept rate for promises due in August 2026?"
  2. 2

    The question is redacted, then checked for scope

    Fixed rules run first, before any AI: is it asking for a protected attribute (gender, citizenship...), bulk personal data, a write action, or data we don't have? Then a small AI classifier double-checks. Out of scope means a refusal with the reason and, where possible, a safer question to try.

  3. 3

    A router works out what kind of question it is

    Three kinds: a certified metric (one of 15 reviewed SQL definitions), a lookup (anything else about the data), or a policy question ("how many call attempts are allowed a week?"). What it understood is shown back as editable chips.

  4. 4

    SQL is built, guarded, and run

    Here it is a certified metric, so reviewed SQL is used (the AI doesn't write it). For lookups the AI writes three SQL drafts and we keep the answer at least two agree on. Every query goes through a guard: SELECT only, 17 approved tables, no personal-data columns, a row limit, a 10-second timeout.

  5. 5

    Final output: an answer with its working

    35.1% of promises due in August 2026 were kept. · SQL shown · tables used · data as of the last refresh

    Every number in the sentence is checked against the result rows (no invented numbers). Groups smaller than 10 people are hidden from analysts.

  6. ✕

    A question it must refuse

    "List the gender and marital status of customers who broke a promise last month."
    Refused: protected ground. Gender and marital status are excluded from collections analytics.

One command, kept nightly, runs every step in about 7 minutes on our machine (more the first time).

  1. 1

    Land the raw files Layer 0 →

    31 source tables are loaded exactly as delivered (every column as text), so nothing is lost or silently changed.

  2. 2

    Clean into silver Layer 1 →

    Names, dates, phones, postal codes and provinces are cleaned by tested rules. Anything unreadable is left empty and listed in a quarantine table, never guessed.

  3. 3

    Match records into people Layer 1 →

    Exact keys first, then a probabilistic matcher (Splink). 3,840,513 source keys become 1,052,300 golden customer IDs.

  4. 4

    One row per customer with lineage. The data contract's rules are checked; if a rule fails, nothing downstream is published.

  5. 5

    Read new notes and calls Layer 2 →

    New text since the last run goes through redaction and extraction; hardship and cash-flow signals update the golden record.

  6. 6

    26 features per case land in the feature store; a parity test proves the live values equal the training values.

  7. 7

    Score every open case Layer 4 →

0Source data
What we were given, and what's wrong with it▾

A fully synthetic but realistic bank: 31 tables covering about 1 million people. Each product system has its own customer key, so the same person appears several times under different IDs.

GroupTables (examples)
CustomersCRM customers (1.02M records), external bureau scores
Productscard accounts, loan accounts, deposit accounts, statements, instalments, monthly snapshots, transactions
Collectionscollections cases, promises to pay, contact history, agents, hardship programmes, offers, channel capacity, model scores
Text and voice760,594 agent notes, 25,000 call transcripts, voice samples
Referencepolicies, procedures, regulations, FAQs, call scripts, incident reports, metric definitions

The data is deliberately messy ("data traps"). A few of the ones we found and handled:

  • Dates of birth typed as DD/MM or MM/DD where you can't tell which (12.7% of CRM). We never guess: reading them all day-first would misread 16%.
  • Month-end dates in three formats (1.25M values). A plain date conversion silently dropped them; we read all three.
  • Masked card phone numbers like 519555xxx42 (58% of cards): left empty, never used for matching.
  • Loan names in two orders (First Last / Last First, 20%): we learn which word is the surname from CRM.
  • A column renamed mid-stream: channel became channel_v2 on 26 Sep (schema drift). We merge both.
  • Provinces written five ways (Ont., B.C., PQ, Quebec...), deleted CRM records still in the extract, notes with lost characters, contacts pointing at cases that don't exist.
  • National Do Not Call flag on 35% of customers. It covers telemarketing, not collections, so it must not block collection calls (a trap a naive system falls into).

Did you clean the data by hand?
No. Every cleaning rule is a tested function, and anything a rule can't read is left empty and listed in quarantine. The quality report lists each issue, how many records it hits, and what we did.
Why not just fix the ambiguous dates?
Because a wrong date of birth can merge two different people. Leaving it empty costs a little matching power; guessing would cost correctness.
1Data product factory
Raw → clean (silver) → one record per person (golden C360) → quality gate▾

Turns 31 fragmented tables into a Collections 360: one trusted row per real person, with every account, case and contact attached.

Bronze: land as-is→Silver: clean→Match: who is who→Gold: golden C360→Quality gate

Matching people across systems (identity resolution)

  1. Exact keys first. Links we can prove: the case's copy of the CRM ID, the deposit account a card's autopay comes from, an identical national ID hash, CRM's own duplicate pointer, and the core-banking customer number shared by deposits and the bureau file.
  2. Then a probabilistic matcher (Splink) on what's left, comparing name, date of birth, phone and postal code. It gives each pair a match score from 0 to 1.
  3. Then a decision by score: 0.95 or more links automatically; 0.80 to 0.95 goes to a data steward to decide; below 0.80 stays separate. A cluster that would hold two different national IDs is never merged.

The golden record

One row per golden ID with contact permissions (most restrictive across duplicate records), hard-stop flags (cease, insolvency, bankruptcy, deceased), current delinquency, open case, payroll pattern and hardship status. Each field carries its lineage. Protected attributes live in a separate table only compliance can read.

The data contract and the quality gate

The C360 is published as a data product with a contract (Open Data Contract Standard, contracts/c360_customer.odcs.yaml): its owner, schema, allowed uses (servicing, the NBA with human release, the Ask, hardship identification) and forbidden uses (marketing, credit or pricing decisions, protected attributes). Five rule groups are checked every night: unique IDs, linkage, match safety, contact safety and freshness. If the gate fails, nothing downstream is published.

You had no ground-truth IDs. How do you know matching works?
Three ways: a person reviewed 300 random predicted links (99.3% correct); Splink independently re-finds 99.9% of the links we can prove with exact keys; and no merged cluster contains two different national IDs.
Why train Splink on 20%?
Training the full 3.8M keys needed more memory than our machine had. Splink learns its weights from patterns that a 20% sample already shows well; it still scores all 3.84M keys.
What happens to the uncertain matches?
Scores between 0.80 and 0.95 go to the steward queue in the app. Nothing uncertain is merged automatically.
Where is the "data product" in this?
The golden C360 has an owner, a documented schema, quality rules, a freshness promise and allowed uses, all in its contract. Consumers read the product, not the raw tables.
2Text and hardship
Read notes and calls safely; spot financial hardship early▾

Agent notes and call transcripts hold the facts that matter most ("lost my job", "I can pay $95 on the 14th", "please stop calling"). This layer turns them into structured, evidence-backed signals.

Redact personal details→Local AI extracts facts + quotes→Quote must be in the text→Signals per case→Hardship status on the golden record

What is extracted from each note or call

Hardship signal (none / possible / clear), promise to pay (amount and date), dispute, cease-contact request, third party on the call. Every value comes with the exact quote it rests on; a value whose quote isn't in the text is thrown away.

Hardship from three sources, combined

  • Text: what the customer said in notes and calls.
  • Cash flow: a rule on account data. Payroll at least 5 days late, pay down 20% or more, or Employment Insurance started in the last 60 days.
  • People and programmes: an agent's hardship code, or an existing hardship programme.

Combined status: possible from any signal; confirmed only when a person or a programme confirms it. Only a person can clear it.

Post-call summary

For the Ask, the latest call on a case is summarised in one line: "Customer reports illness; promised $95 on 14 Oct; next step: hardship plan review." The AI fills in facts; fixed code writes the sentence, so wording never drifts.

What if the AI makes up a hardship?
It can't without a quote, and the quote must literally appear in the note. Even then it only makes hardship "possible", which protects the customer (a review instead of a collection call); "confirmed" needs a person.
Why a local model and not a big hosted one?
Customer conversations shouldn't leave the bank. The local model runs on one GPU, costs nothing per call, and is fast enough for live use.
Why SetFit for the history?
Speed: a small model trained on the big model's labels reads 785K texts in minutes on the GPU, and still reaches 0.98+ hardship recall on held-out labels.
Isn't 0.60 precision on cease dangerous?
That's why text alone never triggers a cease. A false cease would wrongly stop help; a missed one is caught by the agent at wrap-up. A person decides.
3Feature store
The facts the model uses, identical in training and in live use▾

A feature is one fact about a case the model can use, like "broken promises in the last 90 days" or "days until next payday". The feature store computes them once and serves the same values to training and to the live engine.

GroupExamples (26 features in total)
Delinquencymax days past due, times 30+ days late in 12 months, days in current bucket, balance at risk
Promisesbroken promises (90 days), kept-promise ratio
Pay and cash flowdays to next payroll, pay frequency, payroll delay, pay change vs 3-month average, spare cash per month, cash-flow trend

  • dbt SQL computes the features from silver and gold.
  • Feast (open-source feature store) serves them: an offline store for training and an online store for live lookups.
  • Point in time: for training, each feature is computed as it was one second before each past decision. Using today's values to explain yesterday's decision would leak the answer.
  • A feature registry lists every feature, its source columns and its owner. A CI test walks this lineage and fails the build if any feature touches a protected attribute or a proxy.
  • Missing stays missing: no transaction data means "unknown", never 0.
  • No training/serving gap. A model is only as good as the match between the data it learned on and the data it sees live.
  • Freshness is enforced. Features older than 26 hours mean "no action" with the reason shown, never a recommendation on stale facts.
  • Fairness by construction. The lineage check proves the model can't see gender, citizenship, marital status or their proxies.

What's "parity" and why test it?
That the number the live engine reads for a case is exactly the number training would compute for the same moment. We compare every cell nightly; any difference fails the run.
How do you prevent leakage?
Training rows take history from timestamped tables (snapshots, promise status times, the contact-time fields), never the case's current values.
4Decisioning: the next best action
Rules first, then the model, then a promise the customer can keep▾

For each open case, picks one of 8 actions: wait, email, app push, text a payment link, call, call for a payday-dated promise, offer a payment plan, start a hardship review. Seven stages, in this order:

  1. Hard stops (no AI). Cease request, insolvency, bankruptcy or deceased → no contact at all, with the rule named.
  2. Permissions (no AI). For each action: not on the do-not-contact list; consent for that channel; inside the customer's own calling hours (their time zone, daylight saving, Sundays only if allowed); under the weekly contact cap (3 in 7 days by default).
  3. Hardship override (no AI). Hardship possible or confirmed, or a vulnerable customer → only protective actions are allowed.
  4. Uplift model. For each remaining action, the chance the customer pays within 14 days if we do it, minus the chance if we wait. That difference is the uplift.
  5. Kept sizing. A promise due 1 to 2 days after the expected payday, in the fewest instalments (up to 3) that each fit inside the customer's spare cash per pay period.
  6. Ranking. uplift × balance at risk − contact cost − customer burden, within each channel's daily capacity.
  7. Reasons and tier. The top 3 reasons as fixed sentences (from TreeSHAP), and a release tier that says who must approve.
Blockedany hard stop. Nobody can release it.
Redhardship or vulnerability, reductions. Specialist + a second person.
Ambercalls, promises, plans, close calls. The assigned agent.
Greenemail, push, SMS link. Team lead, in a daily batch.
  • Model: LightGBM, one model per action (a "T-learner"), predicting paid within 14 days. Small, regularised trees, because uplift is the difference of two models and noise adds up.
  • Training data: only randomly assigned cases from the bank's past experiments. Randomness is what lets us read "the action caused the payment" instead of "agents called the people who were going to pay anyway".
  • Evaluation: Qini, a standard uplift metric: how much more payment you get by targeting with the model than at random. Compared with the bank's current strategy.
  • Explanations: TreeSHAP gives each feature's contribution in percentage points; the card shows the top 3 as fixed template sentences, never AI-written text.
  • Tracking: MLflow records each model's version, parameters and metrics; every card names the model version and when its features were computed.
  • Safety valves: a permanent 5% hold-out stays on the old strategy (so we can keep measuring), and a kill switch sends every case back to the old strategy instantly.
  • Never crashes into an action: any error or stale data gives "no action" with the reason.
  • Contact the people it helps, not just the people likely to pay anyway. Calling someone who would have paid regardless costs money and annoys them.
  • Promises that are kept. Due right after payday and within spare cash, instead of an arbitrary date the customer can't meet.
  • Safety can't be outvoted. The model only ranks actions the rules already allowed; it can't pick a forbidden one.
  • Explainable: the agent reads why in plain English, with the numbers behind it.

Why are 40% of cards Red?
Red means "a hardship specialist reviews before anything is sent". About a third of open cases already carry an agent's hardship code in the source data, and more are possible from text and cash flow. A protected customer gets a review instead of a collection call: that's the protection working. A rule for customers already on a hardship plan would reduce it, and is the next step.
Why uplift and not "likely to pay"?
"Likely to pay" targets people who'd pay anyway. Uplift targets the difference our action makes, which is what saves money and contacts.
Why does the model never decide hardship?
Hardship is a protection, not a score. The rules apply it before the model runs; the model can't trade it off against expected payment.
How do you know it's better than today?
Qini on the randomly assigned cases compares our ranking with the bank's own recommendations, and the permanent 5% hold-out keeps that comparison live after launch.

Can the system contact a customer by itself?
No. The model creates a proposal. Only a person with the right role can release it, and the release gate re-checks every rule on fresh data at that moment.
What if the agent disagrees?
They can edit the amount or date (within spare cash), choose another allowed action, override with a written reason, or reject. All of it is logged.
6Collections Ask
Ask the data in plain English, see the working, get refused when it's not allowed▾
Redact question→Scope check→Router→Certified SQL / 3 SQL drafts / policy search→SQL guard→Grounded answer
  • Metric questions use one of 15 certified metrics with reviewed SQL. The AI only picks the metric and filters; it doesn't write that SQL.
  • Lookup questions: the AI writes three SQL drafts; we run the ones the guard allows and keep the result the majority agrees on. If they disagree, it asks a clarifying question instead of guessing.
  • Policy and "why did this happen" questions: search over the current versions of the policy documents, incident reports and operations events (e.g. "why did call volume drop on 23–24 Sep?" → the dialer outage, IR-2026-0923), answering with the document and section cited, e.g. (POL-COLL-001 §5.2). A policy beats an FAQ or a call script when they disagree.
  • Call summaries: "summarise the most recent call on case X" uses the redacted transcript (layer 2).
  • Refusals say why: protected attribute, bulk personal data, write action, someone else's case, or data we don't have.

What stops the AI writing a dangerous query?
The SQL guard parses it before it runs. Anything but a single SELECT on approved tables and allowed columns is refused, with a row limit and a timeout.
What stops it inventing numbers?
The grounding check: if a number in the sentence isn't in the result, we don't show that sentence.
Isn't a call transcript personal data?
The Ask only reads the redacted transcripts (names, numbers and addresses already removed), and only summarises one case at a time. Bulk exports and other agents' cases are still refused by the rules, before any AI runs.
Why three SQL drafts?
A single AI draft can be subtly wrong. Two of three agreeing on the same result is a cheap, strong check; disagreement becomes a clarifying question.

On all 96,000 cards, using protected attributes only to check outcomes (never as inputs), with the four-fifths rule as a flag:

  • No protected group is treated materially differently on getting a contact action: ratios 0.93 to 1.00 across gender, citizenship, household, dependants, benefits and accessibility.
  • Flags on "call" come from a rare action (about 3% of cases), where small rates make ratios noisy.
  • Vulnerable customers get a hardship review about 70% of the time vs 34% for others, and fewer calls. That gap is the protection by design.

Source: docs/fairness_report.md, docs/fairness_findings.md.

If protected attributes aren't used, why keep them?
To check fairness. Compliance can measure outcomes by group; the model and the Ask can't see them.
Could a proxy (like postal code) sneak in?
The registry lists proxies too, and the lineage test fails on them. The Ask refuses questions like "which postal areas should we stop offering hardship plans to".

Glossary

DPD
Days past due: how many days a payment is late.
Bucket
A DPD range (1–30, 31–60, ...). Later buckets are more serious.
PTP
Promise to pay: the customer agrees to pay an amount by a date. "Kept" if paid on time.
Roll rate
The share of accounts that move to a worse bucket.
Golden ID
One ID for one real person, linking all their records across systems.
C360
Customer 360: the one-row-per-person view of everything we know.
Bronze / silver / gold
Raw as delivered / cleaned / joined and trusted, ready to use.
Data contract
A file that states a data product's owner, schema, quality rules and allowed uses.
Splink
Open-source probabilistic record matching: scores how likely two records are the same person.
Redaction
Removing personal details from text before anything else reads it.
LLM
Large language model: an AI that reads and writes text (Gemma, Gemini).
SetFit
A way to train a small, fast text classifier from few examples.
Feature
One fact about a case the model uses (e.g. broken promises in 90 days).
Feature store
One place that computes features and serves the same values to training and live use (Feast).
Point in time
Using only data that existed at the moment of a past decision.
Uplift
How much an action changes the chance of payment, compared with doing nothing.
T-learner
One model per action; uplift is the difference between an action's model and the "wait" model.
Qini
A score for uplift models: how much better than random targeting the ranking is.
TreeSHAP
Splits a tree model's prediction into each feature's contribution, for the reasons on the card.
Hold-out
A fixed 5% of customers kept on the old strategy, to keep measuring the difference.
Kill switch
One setting that sends every case back to the old strategy.
Hard stop
A rule that forbids all contact: cease request, insolvency, bankruptcy, deceased.
NL-to-SQL
Turning a plain-English question into a database query.
Grounding
Checking that every number in an AI answer comes from the data.
Four-fifths rule
A fairness check: a group's rate under 80% of the highest group's rate is flagged.
